Post-Gallbladder Preservation Stone Removal Recovery Guide

**Day 1: Adaptation Period**

After gallbladder preservation stone removal surgery, patients typically require hospital observation for the first day. Mild pain at the incision sites is common and can be managed with prescribed pain medications. Patients should refrain from eating or drinking for the first 6 hours postoperatively, after which small amounts of water may be consumed if tolerated. If no adverse reactions occur, a liquid diet can be gradually introduced[1].

**Days 2-3: Early Recovery Phase**

During this period, patients can transition to a semi-liquid diet including congee, noodles, and other easily digestible foods. Physical activity should be gradually increased with assistance from family members to promote intestinal peristalsis and prevent adhesions[2]. The wound dressings must remain dry, and any signs of excessive drainage or bleeding should be promptly reported to medical staff.

**Days 4-5: Functional Recovery Period**

At this stage, patients can begin consuming a regular diet, though still avoiding greasy and spicy foods. A low-fat, high-fiber diet is recommended, with increased consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables to reduce the burden on the preserved gallbladder[3]. Physical activity can be further increased, but strenuous exercise and heavy lifting should still be avoided.

**Days 6-7: Discharge Preparation Period**

Most patients are ready for discharge by this stage. Before leaving the hospital, doctors will examine the incisions to ensure proper healing. Patients should learn how to self-monitor their incisions and understand post-discharge precautions regarding diet, activity, and medication[4].

**Practical Takeaways**

1. **Dietary Management**: Postoperative diet should progress gradually from liquids → semi-liquids → soft foods → regular diet. Avoid fried foods, animal organs, egg yolks, and other high-fat foods. Increase consumption of fiber-rich foods such as whole grains and vegetables[5].

2. **Activity and Rest**: Moderate activity should be encouraged in the early postoperative period to promote blood circulation and intestinal function recovery, but strenuous exercise and heavy physical labor should be avoided. Ensure adequate sleep and avoid excessive fatigue.

3. **Wound Care**: Keep incisions clean and dry, changing dressings as prescribed. Monitor for signs of abnormal conditions such as redness, swelling, purulent discharge, or dehiscence, and seek medical attention if these occur.

4. **Pain Management**: Postoperative pain is typically mild to moderate and can be controlled with medications. Avoid self-administering over-the-counter pain relievers, especially those containing aspirin, which may increase bleeding risk.

5. **Psychological Adjustment**: The recovery period may be accompanied by anxiety, which can be alleviated through communication with family, listening to music, reading, and other relaxing activities. Maintaining a positive mindset facilitates recovery.

6. **Follow-up Appointments**: Attend all scheduled follow-up visits to monitor recovery progress and address any concerns. These appointments are crucial for early detection of potential complications.

**When to See a Doctor**

1. Body temperature exceeding 38.5°C accompanied by chills, which may indicate infection.

2. Redness, swelling, heat, or pain at the incision site with purulent discharge, suggesting wound infection.

3. Persistent severe abdominal pain or progressively worsening pain, which may indicate complications such as bile leakage or peritonitis.

4. Frequent vomiting with inability to tolerate food or liquids, possibly indicating intestinal obstruction.

5. Noticeable yellowing of the skin or sclera (jaundice), suggesting potential bile duct issues.

6. Pale or clay-colored stools and dark urine, indicating possible biliary obstruction.
