## Key Takeaway (30-second read)
Proper diet management after gallbladder removal is crucial. Initially, focus on low-fat, easily digestible foods, gradually transitioning to a normal diet. Monitor symptoms, and seek medical attention if severe bloating, diarrhea, or jaundice occurs.

## Post-Operative Dietary Principles
After gallbladder removal, a patient's diet should be managed in stages to adapt to the body's recovery process. Initially, the diet should be low in fat and easy to digest, gradually transitioning to a normal diet. Here are some basic dietary principles:

1. **Initial Stage (1-2 weeks)**: Low-fat, high-protein, and high-fiber. Avoid fried foods, high-fat foods, and caffeine.
2. **Intermediate Stage (3-6 weeks)**: Gradually increase fat intake, but still control it. Try moderate-fat foods like lean meats and low-fat dairy products.
3. **Long-Term (After 6 weeks)**: Return to a normal diet, but continue to monitor fat intake to avoid excess.

## Food Choices
Different stages of recovery require different food choices:

### Initial Stage
- **Vegetables**: Steamed or boiled vegetables like carrots, pumpkin, and spinach.
- **Fruits**: Soft fruits like bananas and applesauce.
- **Proteins**: Lean meats, fish, chicken breast, and tofu.
- **Carbohydrates**: Whole-grain bread, oatmeal, and brown rice.

### Intermediate Stage
- **Meats**: Lean meats, skinless chicken breast, and fish.
- **Dairy Products**: Low-fat milk, yogurt, and cheese.
- **Vegetables**: Steamed or boiled vegetables, and some raw vegetables can be tried.
- **Fruits**: Various fruits, but avoid high-fiber fruits.

### Long-Term
- **Meats**: Various lean meats, fish, and poultry.
- **Dairy Products**: Full-fat milk, yogurt, and cheese.
- **Vegetables**: All types of vegetables, including raw vegetables.
- **Fruits**: All types of fruits.

## Can You Take Pain Medication When Experiencing a Gallbladder Attack?
**Conclusion**: It is not recommended to take pain medication without consulting a doctor.
- **Reason**: Pain medication can mask symptoms, making it difficult for doctors to diagnose the condition.
- **Recommendation**: Seek medical attention promptly, and let the doctor decide if medication is necessary.
- **Misconception**: Believing that pain medication can completely relieve symptoms and ignoring potential serious issues.

## What Should You Do if You Experience Bloating After Surgery?
**Conclusion**: Adjust your diet and engage in moderate physical activity to relieve bloating.
- **Reason**: Bloating can be caused by gas accumulation in the intestines or indigestion.
- **Recommendation**: Drink plenty of water, avoid carbonated drinks and high-fiber foods, and take short walks.
- **Misconception**: Assuming that bloating is a normal post-surgical phenomenon and does not require any intervention.

## What Should You Do if You Experience Diarrhea After Surgery?
**Conclusion**: Adjust your diet to avoid high-fat foods.
- **Reason**: After gallbladder removal, bile secretion decreases, which can lead to poor fat absorption.
- **Recommendation**: Increase dietary fiber intake, drink plenty of water, and avoid fried foods and high-fat foods.
- **Misconception**: Believing that diarrhea is a common and inevitable post-surgical symptom that does not need special attention.

## How Long Does It Take to Return to a Normal Diet After Surgery?
**Conclusion**: Generally, you can return to a normal diet after 6 weeks.
- **Reason**: The body needs time to adapt to the absence of the gallbladder.
- **Recommendation**: Gradually introduce a variety of foods and increase fat intake based on your recovery progress.
- **Misconception**: Thinking that you can immediately return to a normal diet after surgery, ignoring the body's adaptation process.

## Red Flags and When to Seek Medical Attention
Pay attention to the following emergency situations after surgery, and seek medical attention immediately if they occur:
- **Severe Abdominal Pain**: Persistent or intense abdominal pain.
- **High Fever**: Temperature exceeding 38°C (100.4°F).
- **Jaundice**: Yellowing of the skin or eyes.
- **Persistent Vomiting**: Inability to eat or drink.
- **Bloody Stool**: Blood in the stool.
