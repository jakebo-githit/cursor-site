## Key Takeaway

Deciding whether to keep or remove your gallbladder depends on strict medical criteria, including the organ's function and structure. If removal is necessary, while digestion changes, proper nutritional management—specifically regarding fat intake and gut health—can effectively manage symptoms like diarrhea and bloating.

---

Facing gallstones, many patients ask the same question: **Can I keep my gallbladder?** And if it must be removed, **how will my body change?**

This guide answers both questions: the criteria for preserving the gallbladder and the nutritional roadmap for life after removal.

## Part 1: Who is Suitable for Gallbladder Preservation?

Gallbladder-preserving stone removal is not for everyone. Clinically, doctors evaluate eligibility based on these core indicators.

### 1. Gallbladder Wall Thickness: The Inflammation Barometer
Normal gallbladder wall thickness should be **under 3mm**. If an ultrasound shows thickening, it often indicates chronic inflammation or polyp risk. Preserving a thickened gallbladder has limited benefit and may leave behind hidden dangers.

### 2. Stone Size and Number: Can Instruments Enter?
For Peroral Cholangioscopy (POCS) techniques, stones should generally be **under 1.5cm** in diameter with a low count. Larger or more numerous stones increase removal difficulty and residual rates.

### 3. Gallbladder Function: Is It Useful?
The core premise of preservation is that the gallbladder still works. Doctors assess contractile function via fatty meal ultrasound or nuclear imaging. Typically, an **Ejection Fraction (EF) ≥ 50%** is required. If the gallbladder barely contracts, keeping a "rigid" organ is pointless and increases recurrence risk.

### 4. Cystic Duct Anatomy: The Hidden Hurdle
This is often overlooked. POCS requires passing an endoscope through the cystic duct into the gallbladder. If the duct is narrow, tortuous, or anatomically variant, the surgery is technically impossible.
**MRCP (Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography) is the most critical test** to assess this condition before surgery.

### 5. When is Preservation Not Recommended?
*   Active acute cholecystitis (high surgical risk).
*   Gallbladder atrophy or loss of function.
*   Significant wall thickening or suspected cancer risk.
*   Common bile duct stones (priority goes to the main bile duct).
*   Recurrent attacks in elderly patients with chronic conditions.

The 5-year recurrence rate for stones after preservation is **10%–30%**. This isn't a failure, but a form of long-term management requiring a commitment to lifelong follow-ups.

## Part 2: What Happens After Removal?

If evaluation points to removal, patients often worry: **Can I eat normally without a gallbladder?**

Post-removal, the liver continues to secrete bile. The difference is: there is no reservoir to store and concentrate it. Instead, bile drips continuously into the small intestine in a diluted state. This triggers a digestive adaptation period, especially noticeable with high-fat foods.

### Top 3 Post-Operative Issues

**1. Diarrhea & Bile Acid Malabsorption**
Excess bile entering the intestines can stimulate colon movement, causing post-meal diarrhea. **Dietary fiber (specifically Psyllium Husk)** can bind excess bile acids and is one of the most evidence-supported interventions.

**2. Fat Malabsorption**
Lower bile concentration means reduced ability to emulsify fats. High-fat meals may cause bloating, greasiness, or fatty stools. Follow a **"Low-Fat, Gradual Progression"** principle and consider digestive enzyme supplements (containing bile acids) to help during the transition.

**3. Gut Microbiome Imbalance**
Surgical stress, anesthesia, and antibiotics impact gut flora. This imbalance can worsen digestive discomfort. Supplementing with clinically proven **probiotics** helps accelerate microbiome reconstruction.

### Nutritional Recovery Strategy

| Phase | Dietary Principle |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1–2 Weeks Post-Op** | Clear liquids → Semi-liquids. Total fat < 20g/day. |
| **1–3 Months Post-Op** | Low-fat soft diet. < 10g fat per meal. Eat small, frequent meals. |
| **3+ Months Post-Op** | Gradually return to normal. Avoid single high-fat meals. |

*   **Fat-Soluble Vitamins (A, D, E, K):** Absorption depends on bile. Monitor Vitamin D levels regularly and supplement if necessary.
*   **Long-Term Advice:** Regular meals (avoid fasting to prevent bile pooling in the jejunum), limit refined sugars and saturated fats, and maintain regular aerobic exercise.

## When to See a Doctor

Seek medical attention if you experience:

1.  Diarrhea lasting > 2 weeks, > 4 times/day, unrelated to diet.
2.  Noticeable jaundice (yellowing skin/eyes) or tea-colored urine.
3.  High fever (> 38.5°C) with right upper abdominal pain.
4.  Wound redness, discharge, or unusual secretion.
5.  Unintentional weight loss > 5kg within 3 months.
