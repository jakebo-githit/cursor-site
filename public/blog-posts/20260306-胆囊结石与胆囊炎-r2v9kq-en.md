# Gallstones and Cholecystitis: Gallbladder Preservation and Post-op Nutrition

Patients often ask one key question: **“Do I have to remove my gallbladder if I have gallstones or cholecystitis?”**

The answer depends on symptoms, inflammation severity, gallbladder function, and complication risk. This article focuses on practical, verifiable clinical principles.

## 1) Not all gallstones require immediate surgery

Some patients remain asymptomatic for years. Others develop biliary colic, postprandial pain, nausea, or acute cholecystitis.

Clinical decisions are usually based on:
- symptom burden,
- complications (e.g., recurrent cholecystitis, CBD stones, pancreatitis),
- and preserved gallbladder function.

## 2) Who may be considered for gallbladder-preserving strategies

Gallbladder preservation is not for everyone. It is usually considered only when:
- anatomy and stone burden are technically suitable,
- gallbladder contractility is still meaningful,
- severe acute infection is absent,
- and the patient accepts long-term follow-up.

If recurrent inflammation or high-risk features are present, standard cholecystectomy is often safer.

## 3) In acute cholecystitis, control inflammation first

In acute episodes, priorities are:
- infection and systemic assessment,
- antibiotics/fluids/pain control as needed,
- then definitive intervention at an appropriate timing window.

The core principle is safety-first rather than ideology-first.

## 4) Nutrition after cholecystectomy: gradual rebuilding, not extreme restriction

### Early phase (weeks 1–4)
- low-fat, small frequent meals,
- avoid fried/high-fat foods and alcohol,
- prioritize lean protein and easy-to-digest meals.

### Transition phase (months 1–3)
- reintroduce fat gradually,
- track food-symptom response,
- include soluble fiber appropriately.

### Long-term phase
- avoid both high-fat excess and over-restriction,
- keep weight stable (rapid weight loss may worsen bile metabolic stress),
- continue regular hepatobiliary follow-up.

## 5) Practical checklist

1. Keep a symptom diary (frequency, triggers, duration).
2. Complete imaging + lab correlation.
3. Decide one pathway clearly (preserve vs remove) with your surgeon.
4. Follow a staged nutrition plan post-op.
5. Seek urgent care for warning signs.

## 6) Red flags requiring urgent evaluation

- persistent RUQ pain > 6 hours,
- fever/chills,
- jaundice,
- persistent vomiting,
- dark urine or pale stool.
